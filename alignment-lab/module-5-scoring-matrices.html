Not all mismatches are equal

Replacing isoleucine with leucine barely changes a protein. Replacing glycine with tryptophan can wreck it. A substitution matrix gives every pair of residues its own score, learned from real alignments of related proteins, so that likely substitutions are rewarded and unlikely ones are penalised.

1

From one mismatch score to a full matrix

For DNA, a few numbers are often enough: one score for matches and one or two for mismatches. NCBI BLAST, for example, uses +1 / −2 by default for highly similar nucleotide searches and +2 / −3 for more distant ones. Proteins need more. Their 20 amino acids differ in size, charge and polarity, and evolution swaps similar residues far more often than dissimilar ones.

DNA: 4 × 4

Usually a match score and a mismatch score, sometimes with transitions penalised less than transversions (Module 2).

Protein: 20 × 20

210 different scores (the matrix is symmetric). Each one says how much more or less often that pair is seen in real alignments than expected by chance.

2

How a matrix is built: log-odds in miniature

BLOSUM matrices are built from blocks: short, gap-free regions of aligned related proteins. Every pair of residues in the same column is counted. Each count is compared with what you would expect if residues were paired at random, and the ratio becomes a score:

score(a, b) = round( 2 × log₂ ( qab / eab ) )
qab = observed frequency of the pair · eab = expected frequency from the background frequencies of a and b. Positive: seen more often than chance. Zero: as often as chance. Negative: less often than chance. The factor 2 puts BLOSUM scores in half-bit units.

Real BLOSUM62 was built from about 2,000 blocks in the BLOCKS database, after clustering sequences that were more than 62% identical so that near-duplicates would not dominate the counts. That clustering threshold gives the matrix its number.

Gaps use the BLAST default for BLOSUM62: existence 11 plus 1 per gap position, so a gap of length L costs −(11 + L).

A substitution score measures how often evolution has accepted that change, not how harmful it is in a patient. Glu→Val (sickle cell, −2) and Glu→Lys (HbC, +1) score differently, but clinical severity depends on where the residue sits in the protein and what the change does there.
5

PAM or BLOSUM, and which number?

PAM (Dayhoff, 1978)

Built from closely related proteins and extrapolated mathematically. The number is evolutionary distance: PAM1 means 1 accepted mutation per 100 residues. Higher number = more distant sequences.

BLOSUM (Henikoff & Henikoff, 1992)

Built directly from conserved blocks of distant proteins. The number is the clustering identity threshold. Lower number = more distant sequences.

Move the slider to the identity you expect between your sequences to see which matrix suits them.

BLOSUM62 is the default of BLASTp and a good first choice when you do not know how related your sequences are. NCBI suggests PAM30 or PAM70 for very short queries, which carry too little information for BLOSUM62 to detect them.
