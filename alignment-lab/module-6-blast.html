BLAST: fast enough for a whole database

Smith-Waterman is exact but must fill one cell for every pair of positions. Against a database of billions of bases, that is too slow for everyday use. BLAST (Altschul et al., 1990) trades a little sensitivity for a huge gain in speed: it only looks closely where a short exact word already matches.

1 WordsCut the query into short overlapping words.
2 SeedsFind database positions where a word matches.
3 ExtendGrow each seed in both directions while the score stays high.
4 ScoreKeep the best region of each extension: a high-scoring segment pair (HSP).
5 EvaluateAsk how often a score this high would appear by chance: the E-value.

Run BLAST step by step

The query is the first 40 bases of HBB. The database holds five sequences built for this exercise: one contains an exact copy of part of the query, one carries the sickle-cell change, one is a diverged copy with a mismatch every sixth base, and two are random. Scoring: match +1, mismatch −2, as in BLASTn.

Look at the Seeds step: at any word size of 6 or more, including 11 (the classic BLASTn default), the diverged sequence is missing, although it is 83% identical over 30 bases. No seed means no extension, so BLAST never sees it. Lower the word size to 5 and it is found, together with many short random seeds that waste time and end as poor hits. Choosing w is a trade-off between sensitivity and speed.

Protein BLAST uses neighbourhood words

Exact protein words of length 3 would miss too much, because related proteins rarely share many identical triplets. BLASTp therefore also seeds on neighbourhood words: any three-residue word that scores at least a threshold T against a query word using BLOSUM62 (Module 5). Lower T means more neighbours: more sensitive, but slower.

Rare, high-scoring residues such as W and C give a word a high score against itself, so it keeps few neighbours. Common residues give a lower self-score, and the word may not even reach T on its own.
3

The E-value: is this hit real?

A raw score depends on the scoring system, so BLAST converts it to a bit score S′, which can be compared across searches. The E-value is the number of hits with at least that score you would expect by chance in a database of this size:

S′ = (λS − ln K) / ln 2 · E = m × n × 2−S′
m = query length, n = database length. Here λ ≈ 1.33 and K ≈ 0.46 as illustrative values for +1 / −2 DNA scoring. BLAST estimates λ and K for each scoring system.

Rules of thumb for nucleotide and protein searches: E below 10⁻⁵ usually indicates a reliable homologue; E near 1 means a hit this good is expected once by chance. The same alignment gets a larger E-value in a larger database, so an E-value only makes sense together with the database it came from.
