Why align sequences?

DNA changes by substitution, insertion and deletion. Alignment places two sequences side by side so that their shared history becomes visible and every change can be located, counted and named.

The first 30 nucleotides of the human β-globin gene (HBB) against the sickle-cell allele. One letter out of 30 differs: c.20A>T, which changes glutamate to valine.
1

Position by position is not enough

The simplest way to compare two sequences is to put them on top of each other and check every position. This works for substitutions. It fails as soon as one base is inserted or deleted, because everything downstream shifts by one place.

A single deletion turned a near-identical pair into what looks like an unrelated pair. Alignment fixes this by allowing gaps: placeholder positions that represent an insertion in one sequence or a deletion in the other.
2

Insert the gaps yourself

Tap a base to put a gap before it. Tap a dashed gap to remove it. Watch how matches, identity and the alignment score change. The score uses a simple rule you will study in Module 2: +1 for a match, −1 for a mismatch, −2 for each gap position.

Use your own sequences
Two quantities, two meanings. Percent identity counts matching columns over all alignment columns. The score weighs matches against the cost of mismatches and gaps. Different tools divide identity by different lengths, so always check the definition before comparing reports.

What alignment reveals: mutations in HBB

Apply mutations to the first 60 coding nucleotides of HBB. The tool aligns the mutated sequence to the original and translates both, so you can see what each change does to the protein. Two real disease variants are included.

    changed amino acid stop codon (*)
    Transition: purine ↔ purine (A↔G) or pyrimidine ↔ pyrimidine (C↔T). Transversion: purine ↔ pyrimidine. Transitions are observed more often in real genomes, which is one reason later modules use different scores for different mismatches. An insertion or deletion that is not a multiple of three shifts the reading frame and usually creates a premature stop codon.

    See similarity at a glance: the dot plot

    A dot plot places one sequence along each axis and marks every place where a short window of the two sequences matches. Shared regions appear as diagonal lines. Gaps, repeats and inversions each draw a recognisable pattern, before any alignment algorithm is run.

    same strandreverse complement

    Use your own sequences
    Try window 1 with 1 match required: the plot fills with random noise, because any base matches a quarter of the other sequence by chance. Larger windows filter out chance matches. This trade-off between sensitivity and noise returns in Module 6, where BLAST uses short "words" in the same way.
