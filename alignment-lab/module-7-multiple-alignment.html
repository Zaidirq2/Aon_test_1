Many sequences, one alignment

A multiple sequence alignment (MSA) places three or more related sequences in one set of columns. Positions that stay the same across a whole family stand out, and they usually matter: for structure, for function, or as targets for primers and probes.

Why not just extend Needleman-Wunsch?

Exact dynamic programming works for more than two sequences: the matrix simply gains one dimension per sequence. The cost explodes. Aligning N sequences of length L needs about LN cells.

This is why every practical MSA program is a heuristic. The most common strategy is progressive alignment, used by Clustal Omega and as the backbone of MUSCLE and MAFFT: align the most similar sequences first, then add the others one group at a time.
3

Progressive alignment, step by step

Five short DNA sequences. The method has three stages: compare every pair, build a guide tree from those distances, then merge sequences and groups in the order of the tree.

Use your own sequences
Once a gap, always a gap. When two groups are merged, gaps already placed inside a group are never moved again. An error made early, when the closest pairs are aligned, is carried into the final result. That is why the order of merging matters, and why newer programs add a refinement stage that re-aligns parts of the MSA.
4

Does the guide tree matter?

The same five sequences, merged in two different orders. The sum-of-pairs (SP) score adds up the pairwise scores of every pair of rows in every column (match +1, mismatch −1, base against gap −2, gap against gap 0). Higher is better.
