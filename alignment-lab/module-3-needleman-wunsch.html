Global alignment, one cell at a time

The Needleman-Wunsch algorithm (1970) finds the best global alignment without trying every possibility. It fills a table in which each cell stores the best score for aligning two prefixes, then follows arrows back from the last cell to read the alignment.

1

The idea: solve small problems first

Trying every possible alignment is hopeless: two sequences of only 100 bases can be aligned in more than 1075 different ways. Needleman-Wunsch uses dynamic programming: the best alignment of two long sequences is built from the best alignments of their shorter beginnings (prefixes).

Put sequence 1 down the side and sequence 2 across the top. Cell (i, j) holds the best score for aligning the first i bases of sequence 1 with the first j bases of sequence 2. Every cell is reached from one of three neighbours:

↖
Diagonal

Align base i of sequence 1 with base j of sequence 2. Add the match or mismatch score.

↑
From above

Align base i of sequence 1 with a gap. Add the gap penalty.

←
From the left

Align base j of sequence 2 with a gap. Add the gap penalty.

S(i, j) = max { S(i−1, j−1) + s(aᵢ, bⱼ), S(i−1, j) + gap, S(i, j−1) + gap }
The first row and column are filled with multiples of the gap penalty, because aligning a prefix against nothing means gaps only. The final score sits in the bottom-right cell.

Press Next cell to compute one cell at a time. The panel below the matrix shows the three candidate values and which one wins. Arrows in the corner of each cell record where the maximum came from; a cell can keep more than one arrow when values tie.

3

Traceback: reading the alignment

Once the matrix is full, start at the bottom-right cell and follow the arrows back to the top-left. Each step writes one column of the alignment, from right to left: a diagonal step pairs two bases, a step up puts a gap in sequence 2, a step left puts a gap in sequence 1.

Fill the whole matrix in section 2 first.

When a cell holds two or three arrows, several paths give the same top score. These are co-optimal alignments: the algorithm guarantees the best score, not a unique alignment. Programs choose one by a fixed rule, such as "prefer diagonal", which is why two tools can return different alignments with identical scores.

5

The cost of being exact

Needleman-Wunsch fills one cell for every pair of positions, so the work grows with the product of the two lengths: O(n × m) in time and in memory. Enter two sequence lengths to see what that means in practice.

Estimates assume 4 bytes per stored score and one billion cells computed per second.

Exact dynamic programming is ideal for comparing two genes. It becomes impractical when one sequence is a whole genome or a database of millions of sequences. That is the problem heuristic tools such as BLAST solve (Module 6). Smith-Waterman (Module 4) uses the same table to find local similarities.
